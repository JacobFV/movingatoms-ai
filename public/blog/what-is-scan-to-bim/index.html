What is Scan to BIM? The complete guide

Scan to BIM turns a laser scan of a real building into a Building Information Model you can design, coordinate and manage against. This guide covers what it is, how it works, what it costs and how to get it right.

Scan to BIM in one paragraph

Scan to BIM is the process of capturing an existing building, plant or site with a 3D laser scanner (or another reality-capture device), then converting the resulting point cloud into a Building Information Model: a set of intelligent, editable objects such as walls, floors, columns, ducts, pipes and equipment, each with real dimensions and a position in a shared coordinate system. The deliverable is usually an Autodesk Revit model (.rvt) and an open IFC file. A point cloud shows you what is there; a BIM model lets you work with it.

Why teams use Scan to BIM

Most construction work happens to buildings that already exist. Drawings of those buildings are often missing, out of date or wrong, and measuring by hand is slow and error-prone. Scan to BIM replaces the tape and the site sketch with a measured model. Typical reasons:

  • Renovation and fit-out design. Architects start from accurate existing conditions instead of assumptions. See Scan to BIM for renovation and retrofit.
  • MEP coordination. New services are routed around what is really in the ceiling void. See Scan to BIM for MEP.
  • As-built verification. Contractors compare what was built with the design while fixes are still cheap.
  • Facilities management. Owners get a model of every asset for maintenance and space planning. See Scan to BIM for facilities management.
  • Industrial retrofits. Plants with dense pipework get modeled without shutting down for weeks of manual surveys. See Scan to BIM for industrial plants.
  • Robotics and simulation. The same model becomes a digital twin where robots are tested before deployment. See from Scan to BIM to SimReady.

How Scan to BIM works

The workflow has five stages, covered in detail in the Scan to BIM workflow, step by step:

  1. Specify. Agree the scope, the level of development (LOD) and the level of accuracy (LOA). See how to write a Scan to BIM specification.
  2. Capture. Scan the site with a terrestrial, mobile or handheld scanner. See choosing a scanner for Scan to BIM.
  3. Register. Align the individual scans into one point cloud in a known coordinate system, and clean it.
  4. Model. Build BIM elements against the cloud, manually, with assistance tools, or automatically.
  5. Check and deliver. Compare the model with the cloud, then issue Revit, IFC and any other formats. See how to check a Scan to BIM model.

LOD and LOA: the two numbers that define the job

Two specifications decide most of the cost and usefulness of a Scan to BIM model:

  • Level of Development (LOD), from the BIMForum LOD Specification, says how much geometry and information each element carries, from approximate placeholders (LOD 200) to field-verified as-built elements (LOD 500). Our LOD levels explained guide covers each level.
  • Level of Accuracy (LOA), from the U.S. Institute of Building Documentation, says how close the measured and modeled geometry must be to reality. Secondary sources summarise the bands as LOA10 (coarser than 5 cm) through LOA50 (better than 1 mm), stated at 95% confidence.

A good brief states both, and can vary them by element: LOD 300 at LOA30 for architecture, LOD 350 for the plant room, for example.

What Scan to BIM costs

Traditional Scan to BIM is priced per square foot because the modeling is manual. Published guides from providers give ranges of roughly $0.15 to $0.75 per square foot for modeling depending on LOD, with minimum fees, plus scanning if you do not already have a point cloud. Density matters as much as area: a plant room has far more elements per square foot than an office. Our Scan to BIM cost guide explains what drives price and how to compare quotes.

Manual, assisted or automated?

There are three ways to do the modeling. Your team can trace the cloud in Revit by hand; specialist tools such as FARO As-Built, ClearEdge3D EdgeWise or Autodesk ReCap Pro can speed parts of it up; or an automated service can deliver the finished model. Common mistakes to avoid

  • No accuracy requirement. "Model the building" invites disputes. State LOA per element.
  • Over-specifying LOD. LOD 400 everywhere costs a fortune and is rarely used. Specify by use.
  • Missing coordinates. Agree the coordinate system and survey control before scanning.
  • Shadows in the scan. What the scanner cannot see cannot be measured. Ask how hidden geometry will be flagged.
  • No QA step. Require a deviation check of the model against the cloud before acceptance.

Frequently asked questions

What does Scan to BIM mean?

Scan to BIM means converting a 3D laser scan (point cloud) of an existing building or site into a Building Information Model: editable objects with real dimensions, usually delivered in Revit and IFC.

How accurate is Scan to BIM?

It depends on the scanner, registration and the accuracy specified. Projects usually state a USIBD Level of Accuracy, such as LOA30 (15 mm to 5 mm at 95% confidence), per element.

How long does Scan to BIM take?

Manual modeling is usually quoted in weeks for larger buildings. Automated services such as Moving Atoms deliver within 48 hours.

What software is used for Scan to BIM?

Common tools include Autodesk ReCap Pro and Revit, FARO As-Built, ClearEdge3D EdgeWise, Leica Cyclone 3DR and Pointfuse, or an automated service that delivers the finished model.
